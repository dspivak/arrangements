Pendulum of links

Hang links from a support and let them go. Each link is a bar with a bob at its bottom, and each link hangs from the bob above it: the arrangement of the book's third chapter, nested. The bars are springs, as real bars are. Make them stiffer and the pendulum swings like a rigid one, but each step has to get shorter.

What's going on

The support at the top is fixed. A bar hangs from it with a bob at its bottom; that is one link. A second link hangs from the first link's bob, and so on. Gravity pulls each bob down, and each bar pulls on the two things it joins.

Every bar is a very stiff spring, 1 meter long. Steel bar 1 square centimeter thick has stiffness about 20,000,000 newtons per meter (Young's modulus times area over length). It stretches by micrometers, so the pendulum moves like a rigid one. But the bar also rings along its length, about a thousand times a second, and a step has to be shorter than that ringing or the run blows up. Try steel with a step of 1 millisecond, then 0.1 milliseconds.

With two or more links the motion is chaotic: two starts that differ by a hair end up far apart within seconds.

The math

Each bob of mass m = 1 kg stores a position q in the plane and a momentum p. Told that a step lasts τ, it reads out where it is headed, q + τp/m. Each bar and gravity have costs, and the force on each bob is minus the derivative of their sum at the positions read out. The bob adds τ times its force to its momentum and moves to where it was headed (symplectic Euler).

bar from x to q: k_bar(x, q) = κ/(8ℓ²) (|q − x|² − ℓ²)² ℓ = 1 m gravity on q: m g ht(q) g = 9.81 m/s² one link: k_link(x, q) = k_bar(x, q) + m g ht(q) n links: drop ∘ linkⁿ ∘ pivot, the pivot reading out the fixed point 0

Near its rest length the bar's cost is ½κδ², where δ is the stretch; the form above is used because it stays smooth even if two ends meet.
